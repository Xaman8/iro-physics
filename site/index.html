Inverted Reliability Ontology

Can a failure be predicted from energy that started somewhere else in the plant, and how well does the prediction hold as cause and effect move apart?

IRO Physics models a plant as assets that pass energy to each other through ports and couplings. It groups those assets into envelopes and reduces each envelope to one equivalent asset. Our hypothesis is that envelopes work better than random groupings of the same sizes as a structure for placing sensors and reading the plant. That has not been tested yet. Entropy generation is kept as a power and energy-balance check, not as the measure of damage. Bryant ties damage to the specific process that wears a part out, so losses a plant is designed to have, such as heat a cooler rejects, are not damage. Damage is to be tracked per mechanism, such as temperature ageing, fatigue, bearing life, corrosion and fouling. The toy models still use total entropy generation as one of their damage coordinates. The work so far runs on two made-up toy plants, not real plant data. The first has 11 assets in 4 envelopes set by function. The second has 32 assets, 71 ports and 50 couplings, and its 7 envelopes are found from coupling strength rather than assigned.

In the first plant, pulsation from a pump in one envelope wears out a heat exchanger in another. Both the full model and the envelope model traced the damage back to the pump in all 40 runs, about 62 days before failure. A local sensor on the heat exchanger warned just as early, but it never named the source. In the second plant, a fouling cooling tower was the fault in 20 runs. The full model and the envelope model warned in all 20, a median of 158 to 159 days before the first failure, and named the cooling tower as the source every time. They named the asset that would fail first correctly in only 11 of the 20 runs. The tower itself failed first in 11 runs, and the failure crossed into another envelope in only 6. Neither model raised an alarm in 40 fault-free runs. Local trending raised one in 39 of them. The models still have limits: one fault looks the same as another at the envelope boundary, and combined excitation is over-predicted. The mechanical port carries only shaft torque and speed, so the results say nothing yet about lateral or axial vibration. These results come from toy plants whose true behaviour is the model itself with parameter noise, so they check the method against itself. Tests on data the model did not generate are planned. A scaling benchmark on one CPU core did not support a speed case for envelopes, and that claim has been dropped. No GPU was tested. Solving every asset with sparse math took about 20 ms per update at 10,000 assets, and the exact envelope version gave the same answer but was no faster. On a tightly meshed plant at 100,000 assets, with every factorization redone, the update took 11.6 s against 25.2 s for the envelope version. Speed is now only a conditional, untested hypothesis about approximate envelope models against a deadline fixed in advance.

Next steps: test against the Tennessee Eastman benchmark, the Petrobras 3W well dataset and one nonlinear plant section; check before each attribution whether the sensors can tell the candidate sources apart, and compare with standard fault-detection baselines; and compare the envelope model with random groupings and with a full every-asset baseline on the same faults.
